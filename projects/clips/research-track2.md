# Track 2 research: official clipper programs for long-form YouTube channels/podcasts

Researched 2026-10-22 by Scout. Read-only research: no accounts created, nothing joined, nobody contacted.

## Bottom line (read this first)

- **I could not find 10-15 candidates with verified, public, per-channel terms.** I found about 13 named creators/shows tied to clipping campaigns, but only the *existence* of a campaign is verified for most. The things that matter (what footage you may use, whether clips may sit on your own monetized channel, banned content, credit rules) live in each **campaign brief, which is only visible after signing up** on Vyro / Content Rewards (Whop). I did not sign up, so those fields are **unverified** below.
- **No evidence of any official program** for Joe Rogan, Lex Fridman, Alex Hormozi, Diary of a CEO (Steven Bartlett), Flagrant, Theo Von or Valuetainment. Searches returned nothing from them; do not assume they allow clipping. (Hormozi and Bartlett appear only as guests/third-party mentions.)
- **How these programs work (verified from Vyro and Whop pages):** a brand or creator funds a per-view campaign; clippers cut clips and **post them to their own accounts**, then submit the link; YouTube Shorts is an accepted platform; the platform pays per 1,000 views. That structure *is* "clip on your own channel", which suits William's goal, but each brief may restrict platforms (some campaigns exclude YouTube) or add rules.
- **Biggest risk for a monetized channel:** YouTube's monetization policy says content must be original/authentic and "if you borrow content from someone else, you need to change it significantly to make it your own" (YouTube Help, monetization policies; the reused-content policy covers clips and compilations). Permission from the source creator does **not** exempt a channel from this. Clips need real added value (commentary/captions/context), which matches the existing plan decision on automated captions, but it is not guaranteed safe.
- **Money:** campaign pay is typically about $0.35-$2 per 1,000 views, with a 10% Content Rewards fee on Whop; several campaigns have per-post caps and drain budgets quickly.

## Platform terms worth knowing

- **Whop Content Rewards (primary: https://docs.whop.com/memberships-and-access/third-party-apps/content-rewards):** two campaign types, clipping and UGC; the owner sets rate per 1,000 views, budget, min/max payout per post, accepted platforms, "Available content" (footage access) and "Requirements" (prohibited content, brand mention, length, messaging); the brand approves submissions before pay. So **terms are campaign-specific**; there is no blanket permission.
- **Content Rewards fee/payout (secondary: https://shopiator.com/blog/top-whop-clipping-offers, quoting Creator Terms):** 10% creator fee on CPM payouts, 7-day earning period plus 3-day hold, must be 18+, no botting, rejected-after-approval posts can reverse payouts, do not use Instagram Collab, extra disclosures in crypto/finance/gambling campaigns.
- **Vyro (primary: https://vyro.com/):** see candidate 1. Rates "may change during a campaign" (campaign pages).
- **No evidence found** of a standardized "clips on a channel that also earns ad revenue" clause either way. This is the key question to verify per brief before relying on any campaign.

## Creative Commons option

**How the filter works (primary: https://developers.google.com/youtube/data/reference/rest/v3/search/list):**
- `search.list` takes `videoLicense` with values `any`, `creativeCommon`, or `youtube`. It requires `type=video`, and the docs say CC videos "can be reused in other videos that they create". It cannot be combined with `forMine`/`forContentOwner`.
- The doc page text I fetched states "Quota impact: 100 calls per day" next to "quota cost of 1 unit in the Search Queries quota bucket"; that text is inconsistent. The standard published cost is 100 units per `search.list` call out of a default 10,000-unit daily quota (about 100 searches/day). UNVERIFIED against the current quota page.
- Results page via `maxResults` (max 50) and `pageToken`; ordering by `viewCount` is available, so you can ask for the most-viewed CC videos for a query.

**License and attribution (primary: https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797468):**
- YouTube offers only two licenses: Standard YouTube License and **Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY)**.
- If you reuse CC BY work you must credit: title of the work, author, source URL, and license info (e.g. "is licensed under CC BY"), via text overlay, voiceover, or description.
- CC BY content can be monetized if the license grants commercial rights (CC BY does); YouTube says some owners also require credit.
- A video with a Content ID claim cannot be labeled CC BY by its uploader.

**Is it useful for popular videos? Mostly no (inference, not tested):**
- CC BY is opt-in and rare on large channels; the standard license is the default for all uploads. I did not run a live search to measure how many popular results exist.
- Mislabeling risk: any uploader can tick CC BY, including people who do not own the content. YouTube's own text says only content you can license qualifies. A CC label on re-uploaded copyrighted footage does not give William rights, and Content ID can still hit the reuse.
- The API only returns metadata; it does not provide a download. Downloading someone else's video to clip it conflicts with YouTube's terms regardless of the license (the CC license covers copyright; whether YouTube ToS permits a download is separate and unverified here). YouTube's built-in Editor remix feature is the officially-supported way to reuse CC videos.
- Reasonable use: low-volume pool of properly licensed footage (educational talks, lectures, nature, space agencies, news orgs that use CC), with attribution in description and on-screen, and a quick manual check that the uploader is the original author.
- **Suggested low-cost test (read-only, ~a few API calls, needs William's OK to spend quota):** `search.list` with `type=video&videoLicense=creativeCommon&order=viewCount&q=<topic>&publishedAfter=...` and look at the top 50 for English, high-view, original-author uploads.

## YouTube policy context for a monetized channel

- Monetization policies (primary: https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392): content must be your original creation, and borrowed content must be changed significantly; reused content (commentary, clips, compilations, reaction videos) is reviewed under the reused-content policy; "inauthentic content" (mass-produced/repetitive/template) is ineligible. The page notes reviewers look at main theme, most-viewed videos, newest videos and the About section.
- Implication: even with a creator's permission or a paid clip campaign, a channel made of lightly-edited clips posted at volume risks losing YPP eligibility. The Track 1 decision (varied hooks, on-screen context, human approval) addresses part of it; real commentary or editing is the safer standard. Content ID is a separate system and can claim reused footage regardless of creator permission if a third party owns music/clips inside the source video.
