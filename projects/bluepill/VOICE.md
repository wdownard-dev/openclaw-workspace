# VOICE.md: how The Myth Ledger sounds (v0, 2026-11-14)

Every script worker reads this file before writing a word, and the fact-check pass runs the ban list against the draft. Rule sources are tagged and ranked: [W] William's own edits and reactions beat everything; [F] outside reviewer feedback; [R] reference videos we studied; [M] numbers we measured; [P] our own process lessons. ## The test (apply to every beat)
1. Would a smart friend say this out loud over a drink, to someone who has never heard of this story? [F 11/14]
2. Could someone who never heard of this and wasn't looking for it still stay past second 5, second 20, second 45? [F 11/14]
3. Could any model have guessed this sentence? If yes, replace it with the specific detail only the source has. [A, adapted]

## 1. Who is talking
- A calm, dry narrator who has read the sources and finds the story slightly absurd. Understatement over excitement. Daniel's voice. [R: Genius History, UF] [P]
- Never hype words (epic, insane, legendary, mind-blowing, shocking). The stranger decides it's shocking. [P]
- Calm is the baseline; warmth and humour are what happens on top of it. Calm delivery is what makes a dry line land. Three registers, always in this order of frequency: calm and clear (most lines); dry understatement (about one line a minute, never at the source's expense); warmth (care for a character's choice or loss, one or two moments a chapter, said plainly, never gushing). [W 11/14: "I like calm, however there should be warmer and funnier parts"] [R: Genius History, UF]
- Daniel's delivery stays fixed (style 0.2). Humour and warmth are carried by wording and by where the pause falls, not by performance. [P]
- The narrator may address the viewer. Use "you" to put the viewer inside a picture ("look up"), to share a joke, or to name what they probably assume. Never to instruct, to hype, or to beg (calls to action excepted). About one per minute at most, none in the same beat as a joke. [W 11/14: direct address engages the viewer] [P: guardrail]
- Takes the story seriously and the people in it lightly. Never mocks the source tradition or the people who told it. [P]

## 2. Hooks (first 20-30 seconds)
- Open on a concrete, strange, human moment or a startling specific fact. Not on the topic label, not on the setting. [F] [A]
- Paint a picture the viewer can SEE from the words alone, and leave the next step unexplained. The picture hooks; the plot does not need to be told. (William chose "The sky is a skull" over two hooks that narrated events: one confused him because it painted no picture, one told him exactly what was going to happen.) [W 11/14]
- A vague, exciting glimpse of the ending is allowed and often good ("in fire, with almost every god dead"), because the viewer wants to see HOW. What fails is narrating the events in order. Test: after the hook, does the viewer have more questions than answers? [W 11/14: "it gave a glimpse at the end, but when it's vague and still exciting that's not always bad"]
- The best hook line is a strange, sourced, unguessable image ("The sky is a skull. The clouds are its brain."), then one beat that makes the viewer ask what comes next. [W 11/14] [A]
- Never summarise the story in the hook. A hook opens a question; it does not answer it. (Cyclops V5 beats 1-9 retold the whole cave episode, then beat 12 started it again.) [P, audit 11/14]
- One master promise, paid at the climax (about 90% of the runtime), with the beat that pays it named in the script notes. The hook raises it; it never answers it. [P] [R: pm-claude-skills retention model, adapted]
- A re-hook in the first 30-60 s: one story beat that restates why this matters and pulls forward. Never a meta line. [R: same]
- Stakes within 10 s, an escalation within 15 s. Number-first when a real number exists. [R: UF, Crayon Capital] [M]
- No meta lines: no "in this video", no "let us start at the beginning", no "first, the story". [P] The bridge from hook to story is a story beat. [P]
- Write 8 hooks from different angles (cold open mid-action, the number, the paradox, the person, the open loop), score against the three tests, William picks one. Lock it before the body is written. [F] [A] [P]
- HOOK FORMULA (William, 11/18: the two best hooks so far are Everglades, locked 11/15, and Baba Yaga, locked 11/18; texts in everglades/HOOK-LOCKED.md and babayaga/HOOK-LOCKED.md). Three sentences, 40-55 spoken words, plain words, no adjectives, no setting, no scene-painting. (1) The OUTCOME first, as a flat sourced fact: the extreme result of the whole story ("nearly 80 percent of the adult men ... were arrested or indicted"; "ends with a family burned to ashes"). (2) The small human ORIGIN that caused it, specific and dated or placed: one person asks or sends ("In 1981, the county sheriff asked the federal government for help"; "a stepmother sends an eight-year-old girl into the forest to borrow fire"). (3) A short CLOSER that delivers the answer without explaining it, either by withholding ("Two years later, it came.") or by naming one concrete image plus a flat twist ("What the girl brings back is a skull with burning eyes, and it is exactly what she was sent for."). The viewer now knows WHAT happened and starts asking HOW and WHY. Tests: (a) a stranger who knows nothing about the story wants to keep watching (William did not know Baba Yaga's tale and wanted the rest); (b) the closer adds something the first sentence did not say, so no restating the ending ("and it cost them everything" fails); (c) every line is narratable aloud (no ellipses). Start every hook round from this shape; vary the facts, not the structure. A hook that opens on a scene with no anchor (Baba Yaga R1) fails. [W 11/18]

## 3. Rhythm (spoken, so it has to breathe)
- Mean 10-12 words a sentence, 12-14 sentences a minute, 0.3-0.5 questions a minute. At most one question in a row. [M: refs]
- Fragments land the punchline ("Just gone."). One long sentence per beat at most. [R: UF]
- Three parallel items never run the same length. [A]
- Round numbers in prose when the exact figure adds nothing (about 7%, not 7.4%). Keep the exact one when the story's own number is the joke (22 wagons). [A] [R]

## 4. Specifics (the anti-slop rule)
- Every chapter carries at least one unguessable, sourced detail: a number, an odd object, a quirk of the source. Mark it in the notes with its locator. [A, adapted] [P]
- Facts arrive as plot, not as lectures. Sources become one natural phrase. [R: UF]
- Never add a detail because it makes the scene feel real. If the source does not say it, it is `(ours)` and flagged, or it is cut. (Norse fact-check 11/14 caught Thor's beard, "winter nights", "paw print".) [P]

## 5. Structure and pull
- Chapter titles are story claims ("The Wolf Everyone Agreed to Chain"), not topic labels. [R: Crayon Capital] [P]
- Every chapter closes a small loop (a micro-payoff) and opens the next. [R: pm-claude-skills retention model, adapted]
- One short open-loop line at a chapter end or start, 12 words or fewer ("That is the last time anyone trusts the wolf."). Max 4 per video. [F: pull]
- Origin / "where did it come from" is the closing chapter, not mid-story apparatus. [P]
- Black punchline frames are allowed in the edit as beat dividers; no text cards in the myth lanes. [R: Crayon Capital] [P rule 8]

## 6. Calls to action
- Spoken by the narrator, never on screen, three per video, different wording each: soft (about 0:40), story-tied (just before a cliffhanger, about 5:00-6:00), final (last 20 s): in a multi-part series, point to the next part; in a one-topic video (a character, a single tale) close with a plain subscribe line that names no next video. Max 8-18 words. No begging, no "smash that like". [F 11/14]

## 7. Humour
- Dry, specific, short. About one gag a minute at most. Callbacks to earlier lines. Never at the source's expense. [R: UF] [P]

## 8. Facts and sources
- Every claim traces to a primary or strong secondary source. `(ours)` lines are framing only and say what they rest on. [P]
- Where sources disagree: follow one, name the other once, say so in plain words. [P: Norse]
- Dialogue is flagged as ours and close to the source. No invented quote presented as a source. [P]
- Hedges become short verdict lines ("Possible. Provable? No."). [P]

## 9. Ban list (script-checked in the fact-check stage)
Words and phrases: delve, testament, tapestry, beacon, unlock(ed), journey (unless literal), realm, "in today's", "here's the thing", "little did he know", "buckle up", "epic", "insane", "legendary" (figurative), "stakes could not be higher", "let us start at the beginning", "first, the story", "in this video", "welcome to", "without further ado", "let's dive in".
Shapes: stacked rhetorical questions; "not X, but Y" more than once per chapter; triplets of equal length; any hook that retells the plot; an ending that states the moral as a platitude; em dashes in spoken lines (narration is read aloud).
Structural tells, strongest first (adapted for spoken narration from blader/humanizer v4; act on one sighting of 1-5): [R: blader/humanizer]
1. "Not X but Y" (also "not just", "it's not X, it's Y", split across two sentences). Keep it only when the first half corrects a belief the viewer really holds.
2. A one-line closer that restates the beat before it ("That is the real win."), or a line after a scene that names what it showed ("The message was clear."). Fragments are fine only when they carry a NEW fact or the gag.
3. Sayings that sound deep ("the real question is", "at its core"). State the specific claim.
4. A staged run-up before the point ("here's the thing", "honestly?").
5. Arguing with no one ("to be clear", "you might think") when no one said it.
6. Forced triads; several sentences opening with the same word (unless it is a deliberate beat).
7. Inflated significance: "marking a pivotal moment", "a lasting legacy", "setting the stage for". Myth and history narration drifts here, especially in origin and Snorri chapters. Keep the fact, drop the weight.
8. Shallow -ing tails (", showing how...", ", highlighting...") and vague "connected to / associated with". Name the relationship.
Run `python3 -I scripts/voicecheck.py <script.md>` before every fact-check. It catches words and a few shapes; hooks, rhythm and missing specifics need a reader. [P: baseline 11/14, V5 and Norse draft 2 each had 2 flags, so word-level slop is not our problem]
Add to this list the moment a draft makes a phrase sound like a template. [A]

## 10. The edit loop (how this file grows)
1. A script, hook or cut gets feedback from William or an outside reviewer.
2. Give a worker both versions (draft and edited, or draft and the note) and ask: what rule does the change show?
3. Append the rule to the right section with its tag, the date and the user's own words.
4. Never delete a rule silently; mark it superseded with the date.
