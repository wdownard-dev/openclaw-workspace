# SPINE.md: Baba Yaga, "She never touched her" (video 3 candidate, Stage 1, 2026-11-18)

**Twist (one line):** In the best-known Baba Yaga tale she kills no one; she follows a rulebook, sends the blessed girl home with a lamp, and the lamp, which is exactly what the stepmother's errand asked for, burns the household that sent a child to the border for fire.

Master promise (raised in the hook, paid at beat 10): the witch who is supposed to eat children never lays a hand on the girl, and the people who die are the ones who sent her. Format: told straight, then where it came from (Cyclops model). Backbone: Afanasyev's "Vasilisa the Beautiful" (Wheeler 1912 as the English text). One other tale is used (beat 11, the servants tale); a second (geese-swans) is held as the fallback. Narration is ours and condensed; quoted lines are close to the source. Locators marked (v) go to fact-check; they never appear in narration as hedges.

Source key: **A** = A. N. Afanasyev, *Narodnye russkie skazki* (1855-63); **W** = Post Wheeler, *Russian Wonder Tales* (1912), tale "Vasilissa the Beautiful"; **R** = W. R. S. Ralston, *Russian Folk-Tales* (1873).

## Beats (cold open to close)
**Cold open (about 1:30)**
1. Every fire in the house is out but one candle. The stepsister snuffs it, and the stepmother says someone must go to Baba Yaga for a light. (A, "Vasilisa the Beautiful" (no. 104 in the Afanasyev numbering (v)); W, opening pages (v))
2. The girl is Vasilisa, eight years old. Her mother, dying, had given her a doll and a blessing: feed the doll, ask it for advice, tell nobody. (A; W: eight years old and the doll's instructions (v))
3. The road: a rider in white on a white horse passes and the day dawns; a rider in red on a red horse passes and the sun rises; she walks all day. (A; W (v))
4. At dusk a black rider on a black horse passes, night falls, and the skulls' eyes on the fence light up. The fence is of human bones, the gate posts are legs, the bolts are hands, the lock is a mouth with sharp teeth. (A; W: gate and lock detail (v))
5. Baba Yaga arrives in her mortar, driving it with the pestle and sweeping her tracks with a broom, sniffs, and says she smells a Russian smell. Vasilisa asks for fire. (A; R (v) on the mortar and broom; W)

**The errand becomes a job (about 3:00)**
6. Baba Yaga says Vasilisa will work or be eaten. The chores: sweep the yard, clean the hut, cook dinner, sort the grain from the dirt, separate the poppy seed from the earth. The doll does them all overnight ("the morning is wiser than the evening"). (A; W chores list (v))
7. At evening Baba Yaga calls for her servants and three pairs of hands appear and carry off the grain. Vasilisa sees them and says nothing. (A; W (v))

**The three questions (about 2:00)**
8. Vasilisa may ask one question. Baba Yaga warns that too much knowledge makes you old, so she asks only about the riders: white is "my bright Day", red is "my red Sun", black is "my dark Night", all her faithful servants. She does not ask about the hands. Baba Yaga says she does not like rubbish carried out of her house, and that she eats the over-curious. (A; W (v))
9. Baba Yaga asks how she did the work. "By my mother's blessing." "Out of my house, blessed daughter! I do not want blessed ones here." (A; W (v))
10. She takes a skull from the fence, sets it on a pole with its eyes burning, and says: "Here is fire for your stepmother's house; that is what you came for." Vasilisa walks home by its light, which dies at dawn. At the house the skull's eyes follow the stepmother and the two stepsisters and burn them to ashes by morning. Vasilisa buries the skull. PAYOFF OF THE MASTER PROMISE: the witch kills no one; she gave what was asked for. (A; W, ending (v))

**The turn: the rulebook (about 3:30)**
11. The servants tale: a girl is sent by her stepmother to her "aunt", who is Baba Yaga. Her real aunt tells her to tie a ribbon on the birch, oil the gate's hinges, give the dog bread, give the cat a piece of meat. She does, and in the hut the cat gives her a comb and a towel and tells her to run. Baba Yaga sends the gate, the dog and the birch after her, and each refuses. Her servants say she never gave them anything; the girl did. (A, the "aunt" tale, Baba Yaga cycle (no. (v)); R retells a version (v))
12. What the two tales share, stated flatly (ours, resting on beats 1-11): the door has three rules. Ask only about what you saw outside. Be kind to her staff, not to her. Carry what was given to you in love. The riders, the hands, the cat and the birch are her household, and the household obeys the rules, not her temper.
13. Her job, stated flatly: she keeps one door. In both tales visitors come to her, and she runs the door by its rules. In the Vasilisa tale the dead house has a front gate, a yard, a household and a rulebook, and the lamp she hands over is part of it. (ours, resting on beats 1-12)

**Where it came from (about 1:30)**
14. The hut on fowl's legs is the strangest piece of the picture: a raised house on stumps, like the grave-houses northern peoples built on tall stumps. One flat sentence only; cut if no pre-1930 source is found. (Ralston on the hut and the storehouse-on-stumps comparison (v); Afanasyev (v). Overlap flag: Slavique 10:42 makes the same stump-grave claim.)
15. The road to her is one day long: white dawn, red sun, black night. The riders are her own hours, and the hut stands where the day ends. (ours, resting on beat 8: the riders in her own words)
16. Close: the stepmother asked for fire and was given fire. Vasilisa came home with it and went on to weave linen for a king. The last line returns to the candle. (A; W ending: Vasilisa, the old woman, the linen, the tsar (v))

Count: 16 beats. Sources used: one backbone tale and one other tale (beat 11).

## Notes for fact-check (research notes, not narration-facing)
Stances taken, per William's definitive-stance rule of 2026-11-18. Record of what was chosen and why, so fact-check can verify:

1. **Stepmother put the fires out on purpose.** Afanasyev's text has her extinguish every fire but one candle, which the stepsister snuffs. Chosen over "the fire just went out" (some retellings) because it makes the errand a plan and the ending a payoff. Verify in A and W.
2. **Vasilisa is eight.** W and A give her age. Verify.
3. **The three riders are Day, Sun and Night, in Baba Yaga's own words.** Chosen over "dawn, noon, dusk" paraphrases because the text names them. Verify wording in A/W/R.
4. **The skull burns the household to ashes.** Chosen over lighter versions (they are "scorched") because Afanasyev has them reduced to ashes by morning. Verify.
5. **Hut origin: the grave-house/stump reading.** Chosen over the cloud-house and the storage-cache-on-stumps readings because it ties the hut to the dead without any goddess claim. This is also Slavique's claim; one flat sentence, cut if it cannot be sourced to a pre-1930 text. Candidate locators: Ralston (1873) on the hut; Afanasyev, *Poetic Views*.
6. **No goddess claim, no storm-hag claim.** Chosen over "she was a goddess" (Slavique) and over Afanasyev's storm reading so our video does not mirror theirs and we assert only what the tales show: she keeps one door.
7. **The servants tale (beat 11).** Locator uncertain; Afanasyev has a Baba Yaga tale in which a girl sent to her "aunt" is told how to treat the gate, birch, dog and cat. Identify exact tale number and an English text (R or Wheeler; Lang's *Red Fairy Book* 1890 also prints a Baba Yaga tale). If no public-domain English text matches, replace beat 11 with the geese-swans formula (Afanasyev; stove, apple tree and river each ask for courtesy).
8. **"Russian smell" / "business or fleeing it".** The formula greeting varies by tale; use the Vasilisa text only.
9. **The doll.** Wheeler calls it a doll; in some translations a "poppet". Use "doll". It is not called wooden in our narration.
10. **"Three pairs of hands" with no bodies.** Source says hands appear; "with no arms" is ours and is not used in narration.
