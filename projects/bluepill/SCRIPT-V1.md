## Narration

### Cold open  (est. 0:00, 58 words)

Hundreds of thousands of years ago, an elephant lived on Sicily. It stood less than a metre tall at the shoulder. Its skull has one enormous hole in the middle of its face. In 1914, a palaeontologist argued that hole is where the Cyclops came from. The idea is now everywhere. Today, we check what is under it.


### Stakes  (est. 0:23, 46 words)

If it is right, one of literature's most famous monsters began as an honest mistake. If it is wrong, we have been passing on a good story with nothing underneath. So we will go slowly: the poem first, then the bones, then the people who disagree.


### 1. The poem as Homer tells it  (est. 0:42, 519 words)

Homer tells it in Book Nine of the Odyssey, and he lets Odysseus tell it himself. So this is the hero's own account. They reach the land of the Cyclopes, a place Homer never locates. On a cliff stands a cave, full of sheep and goats. Odysseus takes twelve men to look. The owner is out. His men find racks of cheese, and suggest taking some and leaving. Odysseus refuses. He wants to meet the owner, in case there is a gift. The owner is Polyphemus. Homer says he looks less like a man who eats bread, than like a wooded peak, standing alone above the others. He drives his flock inside, and rolls a stone across the entrance. Homer says twenty-two four-wheeled wagons could not have moved it. That stone is the real trap. Odysseus thinks of drawing his sword, then realises that if he kills the giant, no one can ever open the door. So he waits. Across three meals, Polyphemus eats six of his men, two at a time. Homer gives the detail. We will leave it out.

He finds the giant's own club, a green olive trunk the size of a ship's mast. He cuts off a length, sharpens it, and hardens the point in the fire. Then he offers Polyphemus wine, a gift from a priest of Apollo. It is so strong that its owners mixed it with twenty measures of water. The giant drinks three bowls, and asks for a name, promising a present. Odysseus says his name is Nobody. The present is that Polyphemus will eat him last. It is not much of a gift, but it buys time.

Now the stake goes into the eye. And here is something people rarely notice. Search the whole of Book Nine, and Homer never writes the words 'one eye'. He never mentions a forehead. He says 'the eye', and presumably his audience knew. For a plain statement, one eye in the forehead, we need a different poet. As for the blinding itself, Homer compares it to a shipwright boring a plank, and to a smith quenching an axe in water. That is all we need. The other Cyclopes hear the screaming and come to ask what is wrong. Polyphemus shouts that Nobody is killing him. The others conclude that nobody is. They suggest he pray to Poseidon, and go back to bed.

At dawn, the sheep leave the cave. Odysseus has tied them in threes, with a man hidden under each middle sheep. He himself hangs beneath the finest ram. The blinded giant feels along their backs, never their bellies. Homer even lets Polyphemus talk to his ram, almost tenderly, as it leaves last. Then Odysseus, safely aboard his ship, cannot resist. His crew begs him to stop. He shouts his real name anyway. Polyphemus remembers a prophecy that a man named Odysseus would take his sight. He tears the top off a hill and hurls it at the ship, and prays to Poseidon that Odysseus never reaches home. Poseidon listens. For much of the rest of the poem, he keeps Odysseus from home.


### 2. The skull  (est. 4:16, 345 words)

So that is the myth. Is there anything solid underneath it? In 1914, the palaeontologist Othenio Abel thought there might be. During the Ice Age, dwarf elephants lived on islands across the Mediterranean: Sicily, Malta, Crete, Cyprus, and a scattering of Aegean islands. They descended from a straight-tusked elephant about four metres tall, and island life shrank them. Their bones turn up in caves, and still do. Now look at the skull from the front. The eye sockets are small, and set low at the sides. What dominates is a huge hole in the middle: the opening for the trunk. Abel pictured a sailor, sheltering in a cave and lighting a fire, who sees a skull sticking out of the clay, with one great eye. Everything else, he wrote, was added later. It is imagination, not evidence.

The idea found a champion in Adrienne Mayor, a Stanford folklorist and historian of science. In The First Fossil Hunters, she argues that Greeks and Romans noticed big fossil bones, collected them, measured them, and tried to explain them. Giants, and one-eyed monsters, may owe something to those finds. On the Cyclops, though, Mayor herself is careful. She does not like to say the fossils were the source of the story, 'because we just can't prove that.' We do not know, she says, which came first. Perhaps the myth came first, and the bones merely seemed to confirm it.

Then there is a problem with the most famous version of this theory. Abel claimed that the philosopher Empedocles reported finding such bones on Sicily. Later researchers have found no surviving ancient source that says so. The palaeontologist Mark Witton and Richard Hing put it bluntly: without that story, they argue, the idea becomes baseless. Elephant fossils and one-eyed monsters turn up all over the world, and no ancient text or artwork makes the Sicilian link stand out. The scholars of Greek myth Mercedes Aguirre and Richard Buxton make a similar point. Theories like this sound plausible, they say, but lack a chain of argument linking origin to myth.


### 3. The other Cyclopes  (est. 6:39, 350 words)

Meanwhile, the Cyclops had a different life in a different poem. Hesiod, writing around 700 BC, names three Cyclopes: Brontes, Steropes, and Arges. Thunder, Lightning, and Bright. They forge the thunderbolt for Zeus. And Hesiod is the one who says it plainly: 'one eye only was set in the midst of their foreheads.' These are not cannibal shepherds. They are craftsmen, working for the king of the gods. Same name, same eye, completely different job. The scholar Walter Burkert suggested the smith Cyclopes may reflect real guilds of metalworkers. Later poets merged the two. Virgil puts Brontes and Steropes in a forge under Etna. By the fifth century BC, the Cyclopes are living on Sicily. Euripides sets a comic satyr play about Polyphemus beside Mount Etna. The historian Thucydides lists the Cyclopes among Sicily's earliest inhabitants, then adds that he cannot say what race they were, or where they came from. He also reports that locals believed the smith-god had a forge in the Aeolian islands, judging by the flame at night and the smoke by day. By the third century BC, Theocritus has Polyphemus singing love songs to a sea nymph, and worrying about his one long eyebrow. Even cannibals get a love life.

There is a third strand, and it is real in a way the others are not. Visit Mycenae or Tiryns, and you can walk beside walls of enormous unworked stones. Beside them, even a hero looks small. Pausanias, writing in the second century AD, says the walls of Tiryns are the work of the Cyclopes, with stones so big that a pair of mules could not move the smallest. The walls date to the Bronze Age, centuries before Homer, and people built them. Archaeologists still call the style cyclopean masonry. The wall is real. The Cyclopes were the Greeks' explanation for it. The Greeks did attach giants to things they could not explain. In Sicily, local tradition says the sea stacks at Aci Trezza are the boulders thrown at Odysseus. They are volcanic basalt, about half a million years old. That is legend, not archaeology.


### 4. What we can and cannot say  (est. 9:04, 169 words)

So where does that leave us? Start with what is solid. The Greeks told at least three different Cyclops stories, and kept them apart. A single eye is stated by Hesiod, Euripides, and Theocritus. Homer implies it. Dwarf elephants really lived on those islands, and their skulls really do have a hole in the middle. The blinding story itself is a folktale. Scholars have counted over two hundred versions, from Iceland to Korea, and most think the tale is older than Homer. That does not rule out the skull. It does mean the story does not need one. Now, what we cannot say. No surviving ancient text says anyone mistook a skull for a Cyclops. Other explanations compete: volcanic craters, smith guilds, even a birth defect called cyclopia, though in that condition the eye sits below the nose, not above it. Aguirre and Buxton's verdict is that each has an air of plausibility, and none has proof. The skull theory is a hypothesis. A plausible one, and still unproven.


### Verdict and next  (est. 10:18, 98 words)

So, was the Cyclops real? As a one-eyed giant who eats sailors, no. But the name covers at least four different things: Hesiod's thunderbolt smiths, Homer's shepherd, a volcano's worth of Sicilian tradition, and walls you can still touch. The skull may be one more thread. It is a suspect, not a conviction. Odysseus leaves that island six men short, with a god's grudge. Next time: the winds, the witch, and the other monsters of the Odyssey. Some of them have real-world suspects too. We will see which survive. Subscribe if you would like us to keep checking.
